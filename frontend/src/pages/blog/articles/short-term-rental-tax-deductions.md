# Short-Term Rental Tax Deductions: The Complete 2026 Playbook

There is a version of the short-term rental business where you pay 35% of your gross profit in federal income tax and feel like the taxman is your silent business partner. And there is another version—available to any host willing to learn the rules—where deductions reduce your effective tax rate to the low single digits or even create paper losses that offset your W-2 income.

Both versions involve the same property, the same rental income, and the same tax code. The difference is knowledge.

In three decades of advising STR operators, I have seen hosts with $100,000 in annual rental income pay $40,000 in taxes—and others with the same income pay $4,000. The gap is almost entirely attributable to deductions: which ones they knew about, which they captured correctly, and how their overall tax strategy was structured.

This guide covers every legitimate short-term rental tax deduction in the 2026 tax code. Read it, implement it, and stop overpaying.

## The Legal Foundation: What Makes an STR Expense Deductible

The IRS allows deductions for expenses that are "ordinary and necessary" in the operation of a rental business. An expense is "ordinary" if it's common in the industry; "necessary" if it's helpful and appropriate.

Two constraints limit deductibility:

**Constraint 1: Personal vs. Rental Allocation**
When an expense benefits both personal use and rental use, you can only deduct the rental percentage:

*Rental % = Rental days ÷ (Rental days + Personal use days)*

**Constraint 2: Capital vs. Expense**
Costs that add value to the property or extend its useful life must be capitalized and depreciated over time—not deducted immediately. Costs that merely maintain the property in its current condition are expenses (deductible now).

Getting both of these right is the difference between a clean tax return and an audit invitation.

## The Master List: Every STR Tax Deduction

### 1. Depreciation

**Annual savings potential: $8,000–$25,000+**

Depreciation is the crown jewel of rental property taxation. It's a non-cash deduction—you don't spend any money, but you reduce your taxable income by the calculated annual wear-and-tear on your property.

**How it works:**
- Residential rental properties are depreciated over 27.5 years (straight-line method)
- You depreciate the building value only—land is never depreciable
- You must apply the rental percentage if the property has mixed personal/rental use

**Formula:**
(Purchase price – Land value) × Rental % ÷ 27.5 = Annual depreciation deduction

*Example:* $500,000 property with $100,000 land value, 100% rental use:
$400,000 ÷ 27.5 = **$14,545 annual deduction**

Over 10 years, this is $145,450 in deductions—without spending a single additional dollar.

### 2. Cost Segregation (Accelerated Depreciation)

**First-year savings potential: $15,000–$80,000+**

A cost segregation study is an engineering analysis that identifies property components eligible for 5-year or 15-year depreciation instead of 27.5 years. Under current bonus depreciation rules (60% in 2026, phasing down annually), these components can be partially deducted in year one.

Components commonly identified:
- Carpeting and flooring: 5-year property
- Appliances: 5-year property
- Land improvements (landscaping, driveways): 15-year property
- Decorative lighting and fixtures: 5-year property
- Specialty HVAC for rental: 5-year property

A professional cost segregation study on a $500,000 property typically costs $4,000–$8,000 and generates $50,000–$120,000 in additional first-year deductions. At a 32% tax rate, the tax savings easily justify the cost.

### 3. Mortgage Interest

**Annual savings potential: $8,000–$30,000+**

Interest paid on a mortgage used to acquire your rental property is fully deductible at the rental percentage. Unlike personal mortgage interest (capped at loans up to $750,000 and subject to itemizing), rental mortgage interest has no dollar cap.

**Important:** The deductible portion flows through Schedule E as a business expense—not Schedule A. If you're taking the standard deduction personally, you still get the rental mortgage interest deduction. These are separate.

### 4. Property Taxes

**Annual savings potential: $2,000–$15,000+**

The rental percentage of your annual property tax is a deductible rental expense with no SALT cap. This is a meaningful distinction: the same property tax deducted as a personal expense is capped at $10,000 combined with state income tax. Deducted as a rental business expense, there is no ceiling.

### 5. STR-Specific Insurance

**Annual savings potential: $1,500–$5,000**

Dedicated short-term rental insurance premiums (from providers like Proper Insurance, CBIZ, or Safely) are 100% deductible rental expenses. Standard homeowner's insurance premiums are deductible at the rental percentage.

**Never substitute platform protection programs for proper insurance.** Airbnb AirCover is not a substitute for professional liability coverage, and its cost is embedded in platform fees (already deductible through fee deductions).

### 6. Cleaning and Housekeeping

**Annual savings potential: $2,000–$12,000**

Every professional cleaning between guest stays is 100% deductible as a direct rental expense. This includes:
- Professional cleaning service fees
- Laundry services for linens and towels
- Cleaning supplies purchased exclusively for the rental
- Stain treatment and damage restoration cleaning

If you clean the property yourself, you cannot deduct the value of your own labor. But you can deduct all supplies used.

### 7. Booking Platform Fees

**Annual savings potential: $1,000–$6,000**

Platform fees are 100% deductible advertising expenses:
- Airbnb host service fee: typically 3% per booking
- Vrbo commission: 5–8% per booking (or annual subscription ~$499)
- Booking.com commission: typically 15%
- Direct booking website hosting and transaction fees

**Pro tip:** Your 1099-K from Airbnb reports *gross* receipts—before platform fees are deducted. Make sure you separately deduct platform fees as an expense. Many hosts miss this and effectively double-count platform fees as unreduced income.

### 8. Property Management Fees

**Annual savings potential: $5,000–$20,000**

If you use a property management company, their management fee (typically 10–25% of gross revenue) is 100% deductible. This is one of the largest single deductions for hosts who don't self-manage.

### 9. Repairs and Maintenance

**Annual savings potential: $1,000–$8,000**

All repairs that maintain the property in its current condition (but don't improve or enhance it) are deductible at the rental percentage:

- Plumbing repairs (leaky faucet, clogged drain)
- Electrical repairs (outlet replacement, switch repair)
- HVAC repairs and annual servicing
- Appliance repairs
- Roof patching (repairing leaks, not full roof replacement)
- Pool equipment repairs
- Pest control treatments

**The key distinction:** Fixing = repair (deductible now). Upgrading = improvement (depreciate over time). Installing a new hot water heater to replace a broken one = repair. Adding a second hot water heater to handle increased guest demand = improvement.

### 10. Utilities

**Annual savings potential: $1,500–$8,000**

All utilities provided to guests are deductible at the rental percentage:
- Electricity
- Natural gas / propane
- Water and sewer
- Trash collection
- Internet and cable/streaming services

If the property has internet and cable for both personal and guest use, you deduct the rental percentage. If you install a dedicated high-speed internet connection specifically for guests, it may be 100% deductible as a direct rental expense.

### 11. HOA Dues

**Annual savings potential: $500–$5,000**

If your rental property is in a homeowners association, HOA dues are deductible at the rental percentage. If the HOA levies a special assessment for an improvement (e.g., resurfacing the community pool), that assessment must be capitalized and depreciated—not deducted immediately.

### 12. Advertising and Marketing

**Annual savings potential: $500–$5,000**

Beyond platform fees, all direct advertising costs are deductible:
- Professional listing photography
- Virtual tour production
- Direct booking website design and hosting
- Social media advertising
- Print advertising (local tourism guides)
- Business cards, promotional materials
- Google Ads or Facebook Ads targeting potential guests

### 13. Office Supplies and Technology

**Annual savings potential: $300–$2,000**

Equipment and supplies used to manage your rental business:
- Printer, ink, paper for rental documents
- Computer (business-use percentage)
- Phone (business-use percentage)
- Smart home devices (thermostats, security cameras, smart locks)—deductible or depreciable depending on cost
- Software subscriptions (accounting, property management, compliance)

STR compliance tools like [PermitPal](https://permitpalpro.com) are fully deductible as business software subscriptions.

### 14. Professional Services

**Annual savings potential: $500–$3,000**

- CPA fees for rental property tax preparation: 100% deductible
- Attorney fees for rental-related legal matters: 100% deductible
- Financial advisor fees related to rental portfolio: deductible
- Cost segregation study: 100% deductible in the year incurred (or amortizable)

### 15. Travel Expenses

**Annual savings potential: $500–$4,000**

Business travel to your rental property is deductible when the primary purpose is:
- Conducting repairs or overseeing contractors
- Meeting with property managers
- Performing required safety inspections
- Addressing compliance matters (permit applications, inspections)

Keep meticulous records: date, purpose, miles or transportation costs. Personal trips to your vacation home—even if you briefly check on the property—are not deductible travel.

### 16. Permit and License Fees

**Annual savings potential: $200–$1,000**

STR operating permit fees, business license fees, and registration fees are deductible rental business expenses. These are often overlooked because they're small individually, but they add up and every dollar counts.

### 17. Guest Amenities and Supplies

**Annual savings potential: $300–$3,000**

Everything provided exclusively for guests is 100% deductible:
- Toiletries (shampoo, soap, lotion)
- Paper goods (toilet paper, paper towels)
- Coffee, tea, and condiments for guests
- Welcome basket items
- Beach/pool toys and equipment
- Umbrellas, rain gear provided for guest use

Keep the receipts. Over a year, the little purchases add up to a meaningful deduction.

### 18. Security System Costs

**Annual savings potential: $200–$1,500**

Monthly security monitoring fees and installation costs for systems used to protect the rental property are deductible (at rental percentage). Noise monitoring devices (like Minut or NoiseAware) used specifically to manage guest behavior in the rental are 100% deductible.

### 19. STR Education and Professional Development

**Annual savings potential: $200–$2,000**

Books, courses, conferences, and subscriptions related to improving your short-term rental operation are deductible. The IRS requires that the education "maintains or improves skills required in your work"—which is easily met for STR hosting education.

This includes:
- STR industry conference attendance (travel, registration, hotel)
- Online courses on hosting, interior design, revenue management
- Books and subscriptions (industry publications, this blog)

### 20. Casualty Loss Deductions

**Annual savings potential: Potentially very large**

If your rental property suffers damage from a federally declared disaster (hurricane, flood, wildfire, earthquake), you may be able to deduct the uninsured portion of the loss. This deduction is complex and requires professional guidance, but for hosts in disaster-prone areas, it's worth understanding.

## The Two Deductions That Require Special Handling

### The Home Office Deduction

If you have a dedicated space in your home used exclusively and regularly for managing your rental business, you can claim a home office deduction. This covers a proportional share of your home mortgage/rent, utilities, and insurance.

**The key requirement:** Exclusive use. A desk in the corner of your bedroom doesn't qualify. A dedicated room used only for rental management does.

For hosts managing multiple properties, the home office deduction can be worth $2,000–$8,000 per year.

### Startup Costs

In your first year of STR operation, you can deduct up to $5,000 in startup expenses immediately, with the remainder amortized over 180 months. Startup costs include:
- Market research expenses
- Initial advertising (before the property was available to rent)
- Legal and accounting fees for setting up the business
- Initial training courses

Document these carefully—the IRS scrutinizes the line between startup costs (amortizable) and pre-opening costs (non-deductible personal expenses).

## Deductions That Were Changed or Eliminated: Know What's Gone

**Pass-Through Deduction (Section 199A):** The 20% qualified business income deduction for pass-through entities is still available for STR businesses meeting the income thresholds and qualification tests. It phases out at $191,950 (single) / $383,900 (married) in 2024. Consult your CPA to confirm qualification.

**Bonus Depreciation Schedule (2026):** Bonus depreciation on qualifying property is 60% in 2026 (down from 80% in 2024 and 100% prior to 2023). It continues phasing down to 20% by 2026 under current law, then potentially expiring. Act while the rates are still favorable.

**Entertainment Deductions:** The 50% entertainment deduction (for hosting guests at events, etc.) has been largely eliminated. Business meal deductions are back to 50%.

## Building Your Deduction Capture System

The best tax strategy in the world is useless if you don't capture the underlying data. Here's the system I recommend:

**Step 1: Dedicated financial accounts**
Open a dedicated checking account and credit card for your rental business. Run all rental income and expenses through these accounts. Year-end reporting becomes straightforward.

**Step 2: Category-based expense tracking**
Use accounting software (QuickBooks Self-Employed, Wave, or FreshBooks) to categorize expenses as you incur them—not at tax time. Set up categories matching Schedule E line items.

**Step 3: Receipt digitization**
Photograph every receipt the day you receive it. Store in a dedicated cloud folder or use an expense app. The IRS requires substantiation for every deduction—a credit card statement alone is often not sufficient.

**Step 4: Usage calendar**
Maintain a day-by-day calendar showing: rental days, personal use days, and maintenance days (which don't count as personal use). This is the document that determines your rental percentage—the foundation of your allocation.

**Step 5: Annual deduction review**
Every December, review your deduction categories with your CPA to identify any year-end planning opportunities: accelerating deductible expenses into the current year, deferring income, or making capital improvements that generate depreciation.

## FAQ: Short-Term Rental Tax Deductions

**Q: Can I deduct the full cost of furnishing my rental property?**
A: Generally, furnishings must be depreciated rather than expensed immediately. Residential furnishings are 5-year property under MACRS. Under bonus depreciation (60% in 2026), you can deduct 60% of the cost in year one and depreciate the remaining 40% over 5 years. For smaller items under $2,500 (under the IRS de minimis safe harbor), you may expense them immediately.

**Q: I'm renting out a room in my primary home. How does the rental percentage work?**
A: For room rentals in your home, the rental percentage is typically calculated as the rented room's square footage divided by your home's total square footage. Expenses exclusively for the rented room are 100% deductible; shared household expenses are allocated by this percentage.

**Q: Can I deduct the cost of improvements made before I started renting?**
A: Yes—if improvements were made to get the property ready for rental, they are added to the property's basis and depreciated from the date the property was placed in service (when it became available for rent). Pre-rental improvements are not expensed but are reflected through depreciation going forward.

**Q: My vacation rental lost money this year. Can I deduct the loss?**
A: Subject to passive activity loss rules: if your AGI is under $100,000 and you actively participate in managing the property, you can deduct up to $25,000 in losses against other income. The allowance phases out between $100,000 and $150,000 AGI. Above that, losses are suspended and carried forward to offset future rental income or capital gains upon sale.

**Q: How long do I need to keep records supporting my deductions?**
A: Keep all rental records for at least 7 years. Keep records related to the property's basis (purchase documents, improvement records) for as long as you own the property plus 7 years after you sell it.
