# Holiday Rental Tax: The Complete UK, EU & Global Guide for 2026

Holiday rental tax sits at the intersection of income tax, property tax, lodging tax, and—for owners of overseas properties—cross-border international tax. In over 30 years advising hosts from Cornwall to Crete, from the Cotswolds to Costa Rica, I have seen the full range of what this complexity costs operators who aren't prepared.

This guide covers everything you need to know about holiday rental tax in 2026: what you owe, where you owe it, what you can deduct, and how the most sophisticated hosts minimise their liability legally.

---

## The Core Principle: Holiday Rental Income Is Taxable

Let me start with an unambiguous statement: **in virtually every jurisdiction on earth, income you earn from renting a holiday property is taxable income.** The tax authority may be HMRC in the UK, the IRS in the United States, the ATO in Australia, or the Direction Générale des Finances Publiques in France—but they all have the same view: rental income is income.

The exceptions are narrow (the US Augusta Rule for fewer than 15 rental days/year) and the consequences of ignoring the obligation are severe (back taxes, interest, penalties, and increasingly, criminal prosecution for wilful evasion).

The good news is that holiday rental properties also come with significant legitimate deductions that, properly claimed, can substantially reduce the tax burden. The goal is not to avoid the obligation—it is to fulfil it as efficiently as the law permits.

## Part 1: Holiday Rental Tax in the United Kingdom

### The Post-FHL Landscape (April 2025 Onwards)

The most significant recent change in UK holiday rental taxation was the abolition of the Furnished Holiday Letting (FHL) regime from 6 April 2025. For the previous 40+ years, qualifying holiday lets received substantial tax advantages over standard residential lets.

All of that has now gone.

**What the FHL regime offered (no longer available):**
- Capital allowances on furniture, fixtures, and fittings
- Full mortgage interest deductibility
- Business Asset Disposal Relief (10% CGT rate) on sale
- Ability to make pension contributions from FHL profits
- Eligibility for rollover relief on capital gains

**What replaced it:**
From April 2025, UK holiday rental income is treated the same as standard residential lettings. This means:

1. **Mortgage interest is restricted** to a 20% basic rate tax credit—not a full deduction. For a higher-rate (40%) taxpayer with £20,000/year in mortgage interest, this costs approximately £4,000/year in additional tax compared to the old FHL regime.

2. **Capital allowances on furniture** are replaced by the Replacement of Domestic Items Relief—you get relief only when you replace existing items, not on initial purchase.

3. **Capital Gains Tax** on disposal is taxed at residential property rates (18% basic rate, 24% higher rate) without the 10% Business Asset Disposal Relief.

### How UK Holiday Rental Income Is Currently Taxed

**Reporting:** Holiday rental income must be reported on your Self Assessment tax return under the "UK property" section. The tax year runs 6 April to 5 April.

**Tax rates:** Income tax is charged at your marginal rate—20% (basic), 40% (higher), 45% (additional rate). The property income is stacked on top of your other income.

**Property Income Allowance:** If your total property income (holiday rental plus any other UK property income) is £1,000 or less, you can claim the property income allowance and pay no tax. If it exceeds £1,000, you claim actual expenses instead.

**UK Allowable Deductions (Current Rules):**

| Expense | Deductibility |
|---------|--------------|
| Cleaning and laundry | 100% (direct rental expense) |
| Platform fees (Airbnb, Vrbo) | 100% |
| Advertising and photography | 100% |
| Letting agent/management fees | 100% |
| Property maintenance and repairs | Rental % |
| Buildings and contents insurance | Rental % |
| Utilities during rental | Rental % |
| Mortgage interest | 20% basic rate credit only |
| Council Tax (during rental periods) | Rental % |
| Accountancy fees (rental-related) | 100% |
| Replacement of domestic items | 100% when replacing like-for-like |

**Capital Gains Tax on UK Holiday Rentals:**
When you sell a UK holiday rental property:
- Gains are taxed at 18% (basic rate) or 24% (higher rate)
- You must report and pay CGT within 60 days of completion (not your annual tax return)
- The 50% CGT discount available in Australia and the indexation relief historically available in the UK are not available for residential property gains
- You may offset your annual CGT allowance (currently £3,000 for 2025/26) against the gain
- Losses from other capital disposals in the same year can also reduce the taxable gain

### Scotland: The STR Licensing Scheme

Scotland has introduced a mandatory licensing scheme for all short-term rental properties. Unlike England, Wales, and Northern Ireland (where STR licensing remains largely local/voluntary), Scottish hosts must register with their Local Licensing Authority and obtain a licence before operating.

Licensing conditions include safety standards (gas safety certificates, electrical condition reports, PAT testing for portable appliances), public liability insurance of at least £5,000,000, and compliance with planning conditions.

Operating without a Scottish STR licence carries fines up to £2,500 and can result in criminal prosecution.

## Part 2: Holiday Rental Tax in the European Union

### France: Meublé de Tourisme Tax Rules

French holiday rental tax treatment divides based on your annual revenue:

**Micro-BIC Regime (Revenue ≤ €77,700/year):**
You benefit from a flat 50% abattement (deduction) on gross revenue. Tax applies to the remaining 50%. Social charges (cotisations sociales) apply at 17.2% on the same taxable base.

*Example:* €30,000 gross revenue → 50% abattement → €15,000 taxable → income tax at marginal rate + 17.2% social charges

**Régime Réel (Revenue > €77,700 or by election):**
You deduct actual expenses including depreciation (amortissement). Generally more tax-efficient if your expenses are high relative to revenue (leveraged property, significant maintenance costs).

**French Registration Requirements:**
- All holiday rentals must be declared to the local mairie
- In many cities (Paris, Lyon, Bordeaux, Nice, Strasbourg), a full permit with a registration number is required
- Paris has a 120-day/year cap for primary residences rented as a whole apartment
- Paris additionally requires planning permission to change the use of secondary residences to short-term rental—a process that is now extremely difficult to navigate

**Taxe de Séjour (Tourist Tax):**
French communes levy a tourist tax on guests, collected by the host and remitted monthly or annually. Rates vary by municipality and property classification (1–4+ stars), typically ranging from €0.20 to €4.00 per person per night.

### Spain: Tourist Rental Tax Complexity

**Income Tax for Non-Residents:**
Spain taxes non-resident rental income at:
- 19% for EU/EEA residents (expenses deductible)
- 24% for non-EU/EEA residents (gross income, no expense deductions)

**Income Tax for Spanish Residents:**
Spanish residents include tourist rental income in their IRPF (income tax) return. Unlike long-term residential rentals (which benefit from a 60% reduction on net income), tourist rentals receive no special reduction. Income is taxed at progressive rates from 19% to 47%.

**IVA (VAT) on Tourist Rentals:**
If you provide hotel-like services (daily cleaning, welcome packs, concierge), the rental is subject to 10% IVA. If you simply provide accommodation without services, it may be exempt—but the line is blurry and frequently litigated.

**Registro de Turismo (Tourist License):**
A valid tourist license (licencia turística or vivienda de uso turístico) is mandatory in all Spanish regions. In Catalonia, the Balearic Islands, and many Canary Island municipalities, licenses for entire-home rentals are frozen or suspended pending new regulations. Operating without a license carries fines from €3,000 to €600,000 depending on region.

### Portugal: Alojamento Local Tax

Portugal's Alojamento Local (AL) regime governs holiday rental taxation:

**Income Tax:**
AL income is taxed at a flat 25% rate for non-residents. Residents include it in their IRS (income tax) return under Category F (property income) or Category B (business income, if running the AL as a business).

**IMI (Municipal Property Tax):**
AL properties are reclassified from residential to commercial use, which can increase their IMI (rates). Additionally, properties in "containment areas" (áreas de contenção) designated by their municipality may be subject to higher rates.

**AIMI (Additional Municipal Property Tax):**
Properties with a tax value exceeding €600,000 (aggregate, across all properties) are subject to AIMI at 0.4% (individuals) or 0.7% (companies). Holiday rental properties are included in this calculation.

### Italy: Cedolare Secca for Holiday Rentals

Italy offers an optional "cedolare secca" (flat tax) regime for furnished holiday rentals:

- **Rate:** 21% flat on gross rental income
- **Benefit:** Replaces both income tax and social contributions on the rental income
- **Requirement:** Property must be in Italy; rental agreements must be for fewer than 30 consecutive days
- **Limitation:** Available only to individuals (not companies)

For Italian resident hosts in higher income tax brackets (up to 43%), the 21% flat rate represents significant savings. The trade-off: you cannot deduct any expenses under the cedolare secca—it applies to gross revenue.

**Airbnb and Italian Withholding:**
Since 2017, Airbnb withholds the cedolare secca tax (21%) directly from host payouts in Italy and remits it to the Italian tax authority. If you've opted into the cedolare secca, this withholding satisfies your obligation. If you haven't, the withholding is provisional and you must reconcile through your annual return.

## Part 3: Holiday Rental Tax in Australia

### Assessable Income and Deductions

All Australian holiday rental income is assessable income for tax purposes and included in your individual income tax return. Tax rates are progressive, from 19% to 45%, plus the 2% Medicare Levy.

**The Key ATO Test: "Genuinely Available for Rent"**

Australian deductibility rules require that the property must be "genuinely available for rent" during the periods for which you claim expenses. The ATO scrutinises:

1. **Pricing:** Is the property advertised at a market rate, or is it priced unreasonably high to discourage bookings while maintaining the appearance of availability?
2. **Restrictions:** Are there unreasonable conditions on guests (friends and family only, excessive screening)?
3. **Availability:** Is the property listed on booking platforms during periods you claim it's available?

If the ATO determines the property is not genuinely available for rent, deductions for non-rental periods are disallowed.

**Apportionment:**
When the property is used partly for personal purposes and partly for rental, expenses must be apportioned. The standard method is:

Rental weeks ÷ Total weeks used = Deductible percentage

This percentage applies to all shared expenses (mortgage interest, insurance, rates, utilities, maintenance).

**GST:**
If your annual STR turnover exceeds $75,000 (from all income sources), you must register for GST, charge 10% on rental income, and lodge quarterly BAS statements. Input tax credits are available for GST paid on rental-related expenses.

**Land Tax:**
Several Australian states impose land tax on properties not used as a primary residence. Holiday rental properties are typically subject to land tax, with rates varying by state and land value.

### CGT on Australian Holiday Rentals

When you sell an Australian holiday rental property:
- The 50% CGT discount applies if held for more than 12 months
- The discount applies to the proportion of the gain relating to the income-producing use (not the personal-use portion)
- The main residence exemption does not apply to properties that have never been your primary residence
- Depreciation claimed during the rental period is not recaptured at a higher rate (unlike the US)—it simply reduces the cost base, increasing the capital gain

## The Universal Deductions That Apply Across Jurisdictions

Despite the jurisdictional differences in tax rates and specific rules, these expense categories are deductible (in some form) in virtually every major holiday rental market:

**Property maintenance and repairs:** Keeping the property in lettable condition is universally deductible.

**Management and agency fees:** Agent commissions and management fees are universally deductible as direct business costs.

**Cleaning costs:** Guest-related cleaning is a direct rental cost, deductible in full.

**Advertising and platform fees:** Airbnb fees, Vrbo fees, photography, and direct advertising costs are universally deductible.

**Insurance:** Holiday home insurance premiums are deductible in all major jurisdictions.

**Depreciation/capital allowances:** The mechanism differs by country, but all major jurisdictions allow some form of cost recovery on the building and its contents.

## Practical Tax Planning for Holiday Rental Owners in 2026

### Strategy 1: Track Personal Use Meticulously

The single most important record you can keep is a day-by-day log of personal use vs. rental use. This determines your deductible percentage in virtually every jurisdiction. Even a casual weekend visit by family members can shift the calculation meaningfully.

### Strategy 2: Get Local Specialist Advice in Every Jurisdiction

Do not assume the rules that apply to your domestic holiday rental apply to your overseas one. A UK accountant who does not practice French tax law cannot reliably advise you on your Provence gîte. Get jurisdiction-specific advice for every country where you own rental property.

### Strategy 3: Consider Ownership Structure Carefully

In France, owning property through a Société Civile Immobilière (SCI) can simplify inheritance and may offer CGT benefits on sale. In Spain, corporate ownership structures are sometimes used by non-residents to access expense deductibility. In the UK, limited company structures have become more common post-FHL abolition. Get advice before you buy—not after.

### Strategy 4: Understand and Use Relief Mechanisms

Many countries offer relief mechanisms to prevent true double taxation on cross-border rental income. Foreign Tax Credits (US), Double Taxation Agreement credits (UK), and similar mechanisms ensure you don't pay tax twice on the same income. These reliefs require proper filing to claim.

## FAQ: Holiday Rental Tax

**Q: I never received any tax forms relating to my holiday rental. Does that mean I don't owe tax?**
A: No. Tax obligations exist regardless of whether you receive a form. The absence of a form (a 1099-K, a UK HMRC notice, or a French tax statement) does not exempt you from the obligation to report and pay tax on rental income. If you've been operating without reporting, consider making a voluntary disclosure to the relevant tax authority—the treatment of voluntary disclosures is almost always more favourable than when evasion is discovered.

**Q: My holiday rental made a loss this year. Can I offset it against my other income?**
A: This depends on your jurisdiction and circumstances. In the UK, residential property losses are ring-fenced against future property income. In the US, the passive activity rules limit loss deductions for most landlords (with exceptions for low-to-middle income active participants). In Australia, negative gearing allows property losses to offset other income for Australian residents. In France, losses under the régime réel can be carried forward for up to 10 years.

**Q: How are short-term rental platforms reporting my income to tax authorities?**
A: Under the OECD's Model Rules for Reporting by Platform Operators (DAC7 in the EU, similar rules in the US and UK), booking platforms are now required to collect and report host income data to tax authorities annually. In the EU, this data is automatically shared between member states. The practical effect is that your income on Airbnb, Vrbo, and other major platforms is known to your tax authority. Discrepancies between platform-reported income and your tax return are flagged for review.
