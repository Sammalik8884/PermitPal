# Vacation Rental Tax Rules: The Complete 2026 Guide for Property Owners

The vacation rental tax rules are among the most nuanced provisions in the entire US tax code—and among the most consequential for property owners who get them wrong.

I have seen hosts owe $60,000 in back taxes because they didn't understand the difference between the vacation home rules and the rental property rules. I have seen others with nearly identical properties and rental income pay almost nothing in federal income tax because they structured their activity correctly.

This guide is the complete breakdown of vacation rental tax rules in 2026. I've written it for property owners who want to understand not just *what* the rules are, but *why* they exist and how to use them to your advantage.

---

## The Foundation: Two Sets of Rules, One Critical Distinction

The IRS does not apply a single uniform tax regime to all vacation rental properties. Instead, there are fundamentally different rules depending on how you use your property:

**Set 1: The Vacation Home Rules** — Apply when the property is used for personal enjoyment AND rented to others. These rules limit your deductions and prevent rental losses from offsetting your other income.

**Set 2: The Rental Property Rules** — Apply when the property is used primarily as a rental with minimal personal use. These rules allow full deductions and potentially allow rental losses to offset other income.

**The Special Case: The Augusta Rule** — A narrow exception that makes income from short-term rentals (14 days or fewer per year) completely tax-free.

Which set of rules applies to your property depends on a precise calculation based on how many days you personally use it versus how many days you rent it. Understanding this calculation—and managing it proactively—is the single most important tax planning decision you make as a vacation rental owner.

---

## The 14-Day / 10% Test: The Rule That Determines Everything

The IRS uses a two-part test to determine how your vacation rental is classified:

**The Test:**
Your property is classified as a "vacation home" (rather than a rental property) if your personal use exceeds the GREATER of:
- 14 days, OR
- 10% of the total days the property is rented at fair market rates

If your personal use is at or below this threshold, the property is classified as a rental property. If it exceeds the threshold, it's classified as a vacation home.

### Worked Examples

**Example 1: Full Rental Property**
- Days rented: 200
- Days personally used: 14
- Threshold: Greater of 14 days or 10% of 200 (= 20 days) = 20 days
- Personal use (14) is BELOW threshold (20)
- Result: **Rental property rules apply** ✅

**Example 2: Vacation Home**
- Days rented: 100
- Days personally used: 25
- Threshold: Greater of 14 days or 10% of 100 (= 10 days) = 14 days
- Personal use (25) is ABOVE threshold (14)
- Result: **Vacation home rules apply** ⚠️

**Example 3: Borderline Case**
- Days rented: 150
- Days personally used: 15
- Threshold: Greater of 14 days or 10% of 150 (= 15 days) = 15 days
- Personal use (15) is EQUAL to threshold (15)
- Result: **Rental property rules apply** (equal to, not exceeding, the threshold) ✅

The borderline case matters enormously in practice. Careful management of your personal use days can determine which tax regime applies.

## What Counts as "Personal Use"?

This is where the rules get surprisingly complex. Personal use days include:

**Days you use the property for personal enjoyment.** Any day you (or your family) use the property for personal enjoyment counts. This includes both overnight stays and day trips.

**Days used by family members at below-market rates.** If your adult child stays at the property for a week and pays you nothing (or pays below market rate), those days count as your personal use.

**Days used by anyone at below-market rates.** If you let a friend stay at the property in exchange for a favor, or for free, those days count as personal use.

**Days traded under a swap arrangement.** If you swap your beach house for someone else's ski chalet, those days count as personal use at both properties.

**What does NOT count as personal use:**
- Days you spend at the property doing repairs, maintenance, or renovation (as long as you don't use the property for personal enjoyment on those same days)
- Days the property is rented at fair market rates (obviously)
- Days the property sits empty while available for rent but not rented

The "repair exception" is a significant tax planning opportunity. If you spend a week at your beach house replacing decking and doing maintenance, those days don't count as personal use—even though you're physically present. Keep detailed records of the work performed.

## Tax Rules When Your Property Is a "Vacation Home"

If the 14-day / 10% test results in vacation home classification, here are the specific rules that apply:

### Rule 1: All Rental Income Must Be Reported

There is no exclusion or reduced reporting for vacation homes. All rental income is taxable and must be reported on Schedule E.

### Rule 2: Expenses Are Deductible Only in Proportion to Rental Days

You calculate your "rental percentage" as:
Rental days ÷ (Rental days + Personal use days)

Only this percentage of shared expenses (mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance) is deductible as rental expenses.

**Example:**
- Rental days: 100
- Personal use days: 25
- Rental percentage: 100 ÷ 125 = 80%
- Mortgage interest: $20,000 × 80% = $16,000 deductible as rental expense
- Remaining 20% ($4,000) goes to Schedule A as personal mortgage interest deduction

### Rule 3: Rental Losses Cannot Offset Other Income

If your rental expenses (at the rental percentage) exceed your rental income, you have a rental loss. Under the vacation home rules, this loss cannot be deducted against your other income (wages, business income, etc.).

The unused loss is suspended and can only be used to offset future rental income from the same property, or deducted when the property is sold.

### Rule 4: Expense Deduction Order (The IRS-Mandated Sequence)

For vacation homes, the IRS mandates that you deduct expenses in a specific order:

**Tier 1:** Deduct allocated mortgage interest and property taxes first (these are allowed up to the amount of gross rental income)

**Tier 2:** Then deduct operating expenses (insurance, utilities, management fees, cleaning) up to any remaining gross rental income

**Tier 3:** Then deduct depreciation up to any remaining gross rental income

This ordering maximizes the chance that you'll reach zero rental income before you've deducted everything—meaning the expense excess is trapped as a suspended loss. It's one reason the vacation home rules are unfavorable compared to rental property rules.

## Tax Rules When Your Property Is a "Rental Property"

If you keep personal use at or below the 14-day / 10% threshold, your vacation rental is treated as a rental property—a significantly more favorable tax classification.

### Rule 1: All Rental Income Is Reported

Same as vacation home—rental income must be reported.

### Rule 2: All Rental Expenses Are Deductible

Unlike vacation home treatment, all legitimate rental expenses are fully deductible without the proportional limitation. Mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance, utilities, maintenance, depreciation—all deductible without allocating a "personal use" portion (because personal use is minimal).

### Rule 3: Rental Losses May Offset Other Income

This is the critical advantage. If rental expenses exceed rental income (creating a rental loss), that loss may be deductible against other income:

**Under the $25,000 Passive Activity Exception:**
If your AGI is under $100,000 and you actively participate in managing the rental (making management decisions, not necessarily doing physical labor), you can deduct up to $25,000 in rental losses per year against your other income. This allowance phases out between $100,000 and $150,000 AGI.

**Under the Real Estate Professional Exception:**
If you or your spouse qualifies as a real estate professional (750+ hours/year in real estate activities, more than 50% of working time in real estate), the passive activity rules don't apply at all. Rental losses can offset other income without dollar limit.

**Under the Short-Term Rental Exception:**
If your average rental period is 7 days or fewer (which is common for vacation rentals) AND you materially participate in the activity (500+ hours/year managing properties, or meeting other material participation tests), the rental activity is not classified as passive. Losses offset other income without the $25,000 limitation or real estate professional qualification.

This short-term rental exception is one of the most powerful and underutilized provisions in the tax code for vacation rental operators.

## The Augusta Rule (The Tax-Free Exception)

If you rent your vacation property for **14 days or fewer** in the entire calendar year, all rental income is excluded from taxable income under IRC Section 280A(g).

**This is completely legitimate.** The rule has been in the tax code since 1976.

**How to use it:**
- Rent during premium periods (major events, holidays, peak season) at premium rates
- Stay strictly under 15 total rental days
- Collect the income, pay zero income tax on it

**What you give up:**
- You cannot deduct any rental expenses (cleaning, management fees, advertising)
- The property is treated as a pure personal residence for tax purposes

**When it makes sense:**
- Property in a high-demand market where you can achieve very high nightly rates in a short rental period
- Host who wants occasional rental income without the complexity of full rental property reporting
- The property value is so high that the tax savings from the exclusion outweigh the lost deductions

## State Vacation Rental Tax Rules

Federal rules are only part of the picture. Every state has its own tax treatment of vacation rental income:

### States With Specific STR Tax Provisions

**California:**
California has no special vacation home rules at the state level—it follows federal classification (vacation home vs. rental property) for state income tax purposes. State income tax rates are up to 13.3%.

California also has aggressive local TOT enforcement. The California Department of Tax and Fee Administration (CDTFA) actively audits STR operators, and many California counties now receive Airbnb data directly.

**Florida:**
Florida has no state income tax, but STR operators face:
- Florida Sales Tax (6%) on short-term rental income
- County Tourist Development Tax (TDT): 2–6% depending on county
- Some counties add additional local taxes

Total Florida lodging tax burden can reach 12–14% in some counties (e.g., Miami-Dade, Orange County/Orlando).

**Hawaii:**
Hawaii's vacation rental tax is among the most complex in the US:
- Hawaii General Excise Tax (GET): 4% (4.5% in Oahu) on gross rental income—this is a tax on the host, not just a pass-through
- Hawaii Transient Accommodations Tax (TAT): 10.25% on gross rental income
- Combined effective rate: 14.25%–14.75% before state/federal income tax

Hawaii also requires a state permit and county-level permits in most cases.

**Maine:**
Maine has specific short-term rental tax provisions:
- All STR income is subject to Maine state income tax (5.8%)
- Maine sales tax applies to rental income at 9% (lodging rate)
- Registration required with Maine Revenue Services

**Monroe County, Florida (Florida Keys):**
Monroe County has its own additional Tourist Development Tax rate on top of Florida state sales tax, creating a combined lodging tax burden among the highest in the country.

## Vacation Rental Tax Reporting: What Goes Where

**Schedule E:** Reports rental income and expenses for most vacation rental properties. One Schedule E per property (or up to 3 per Schedule E form).

**Schedule A:** The personal portion of mortgage interest and property taxes from a vacation home may be deductible as itemized deductions (subject to SALT cap and mortgage interest limits).

**Form 4562:** Used to calculate and report depreciation.

**Form 8582:** Used to calculate passive activity loss limitations.

**Form 8960:** Used to calculate the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT) if applicable.

## Managing Your Vacation Rental Tax Position: Strategic Moves

### Move 1: Track Personal Use Days Religiously

The difference between vacation home and rental property treatment can be a single day's difference in personal use. Keep a calendar. Document every day you or any family member uses the property. Document every day spent on maintenance (which doesn't count as personal use).

### Move 2: Use the Repair Exception Strategically

If you want to be physically present at the property without counting those days as personal use, structure the visit as a maintenance or repair visit. Do actual, documented maintenance work. Don't mix maintenance days with vacation days—the IRS scrutinizes this.

### Move 3: Consider the Short-Term Rental Exception

If your average rental period is 7 days or fewer and you materially participate in operations, the passive activity rules don't apply. Losses can offset your other income. For high-income hosts with active rental operations, qualifying for this exception can save tens of thousands annually.

### Move 4: Maximize Depreciation in the First Year

In your first year of rental operations, commission a cost segregation study. The accelerated depreciation generates significant first-year deductions. At 60% bonus depreciation in 2026, a $500,000 property's cost segregation study can generate $50,000–$90,000 in additional first-year deductions.

### Move 5: Plan Your Sale with a 1031 Exchange

When you eventually sell your vacation rental, a 1031 exchange allows you to defer capital gains tax and depreciation recapture by rolling proceeds into another like-kind property. This is one of the most powerful wealth-building strategies available in real estate.

## FAQ: Vacation Rental Tax Rules

**Q: What if I inherit a vacation property and then start renting it?**
A: The property gets a "stepped-up basis" to its fair market value at the date of inheritance for capital gains purposes—eliminating any gain accrued during the decedent's lifetime. For depreciation purposes, you start a new depreciation schedule based on the stepped-up fair market value at the date of inheritance. This is extremely favorable and is one of the strongest arguments for holding appreciated real estate until death rather than selling it.

**Q: Can I deduct mortgage points paid on a vacation rental property?**
A: Points paid to obtain a mortgage on a rental property are generally deductible, but they must be amortized over the life of the loan (not deducted in full in the year paid, unlike points on a primary residence purchase mortgage). Points paid on a refinance are always amortized.

**Q: My vacation rental is managed entirely by a management company—does that affect my classification?**
A: If a management company handles everything and you're completely hands-off, you may fail the "active participation" test required for the $25,000 passive loss allowance. You might also fail the "material participation" tests required for the short-term rental exception. Work with your CPA to structure your involvement appropriately if these exceptions matter to your tax situation.

**Q: How does the vacation home classification affect me when I sell?**
A: Regardless of whether the property was classified as a vacation home or rental property during ownership, capital gains tax applies on sale. If the property qualifies as your primary residence for at least 2 of the last 5 years, you may exclude up to $250,000 ($500,000 married) in gain from taxable income under IRC Section 121. But depreciation recapture (at 25%) applies to any depreciation claimed, regardless of the primary residence exclusion.
