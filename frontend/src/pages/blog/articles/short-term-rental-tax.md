## How Short-Term Rental Income Is Taxed: The Basics

Short-term rental income is subject to federal income tax, state income tax (in most states), and potentially local lodging taxes. But the specific tax treatment depends critically on two factors:

1. **How many days you rent the property**
2. **How many days you personally use the property**

The IRS has constructed a framework based on these two factors that determines whether your rental activity is treated as a business, a passive investment, or—in one special case—entirely excluded from taxable income.

### Category 1: The Augusta Rule (Fewest Than 15 Rental Days)

If you rent your property for **fewer than 15 days in the calendar year**, something remarkable happens: the rental income is completely tax-free under IRC Section 280A(g). You don't report it. You don't pay tax on it. It doesn't even count as income.

This applies even if you rent your property at an astronomical nightly rate. Fourteen nights at $5,000 per night = $70,000 in tax-free income.

The trade-off: you cannot deduct any rental-related expenses. The property is treated as a pure personal residence for tax purposes.

For hosts who only want to rent occasionally—during major local events, holidays, or peak seasons—this can be an extraordinarily tax-efficient strategy.

### Category 2: Rental Property (15+ Days Rental, Minimal Personal Use)

If you rent for 15 or more days AND your personal use does not exceed the greater of 14 days or 10% of rental days, the property is classified as a rental property (not a vacation home).

This is the most favorable classification for most active STR hosts because:
- All rental income must be reported (no exclusion)
- But ALL legitimate rental expenses are deductible
- Rental losses may be deductible against other income (subject to passive activity rules)
- Depreciation can be claimed on the full rental portion of the property

### Category 3: Vacation Home (15+ Days Rental, Significant Personal Use)

If you rent for 15 or more days AND your personal use exceeds 14 days or 10% of rental days (whichever is greater), the IRS classifies the property as a "vacation home."

Under this classification:
- All rental income must be reported
- Rental expenses can only be deducted in proportion to rental days (not total days used)
- Rental losses cannot be used to offset other income
- The deduction limitation can create a "phantom income" situation where you owe tax even though the property barely broke even

Understanding which category you fall into—and potentially managing your personal use days to achieve a favorable classification—is one of the most impactful STR tax planning decisions you can make.

## Short-Term Rental Tax Rate: What Are You Actually Paying?

The tax rate on short-term rental income depends on:

**1. Income Classification**
- Passive rental income (Schedule E): taxed at ordinary income tax rates (10%–37%)
- Active business income (Schedule C): taxed at ordinary rates PLUS 15.3% self-employment tax

**2. Your Total Income**
The federal income tax rate is progressive. Your rental income is stacked on top of your other income. If you already earn $200,000 from your job and earn an additional $50,000 from STRs, that $50,000 is taxed at the 32% or 35% marginal rate.

**3. Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT)**
If your income exceeds $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly), a 3.8% NIIT surtax applies to passive rental income.

**4. State and Local Taxes**
Add state income tax (0% in Florida, Texas, and other no-income-tax states; up to 13.3% in California) and you get to the total marginal rate on your rental income.

For a California host earning $300,000 total income including STR income, the effective marginal rate on each additional dollar of rental income can approach 55% (37% federal + 13.3% state + 3.8% NIIT). Proper tax planning is not optional at these income levels—it's essential.

## The Tax Deductions That Actually Move the Needle

This is where money is made or lost. Here are the deductions that have the biggest impact for the STR hosts I've worked with:

### 1. Depreciation ($8,000–$20,000+ per year)

For a property worth $400,000 (excluding land), residential depreciation at 1/27.5 per year generates a $14,545 annual non-cash deduction. This is money you don't spend but gets deducted from your taxable income every year.

Over the lifetime of your property, this adds up to the full building value—a deduction of hundreds of thousands of dollars.

**Accelerated depreciation through cost segregation:**
A cost segregation study identifies property components that qualify for accelerated depreciation (5-year or 15-year property instead of 27.5-year). In the first year alone, a well-conducted cost segregation study on a $500,000 vacation rental property can generate $80,000–$150,000 in additional first-year depreciation deductions.

If you're in the 32% tax bracket, that additional $100,000 deduction saves you $32,000 in federal taxes. The cost segregation study costs $3,000–$10,000. The math is straightforward.

### 2. Mortgage Interest ($10,000–$30,000+ per year)

Interest paid on a mortgage secured by a rental property is deductible as a rental expense (not subject to the consumer mortgage interest deduction limits that apply to personal residences). 

If your property is used for both personal and rental purposes, you deduct the rental percentage of your mortgage interest.

### 3. Property Taxes ($3,000–$15,000+ per year)

The rental percentage of your property taxes is deductible as a rental expense. Unlike the $10,000 SALT cap that applies to personal property tax deductions, the rental portion of property taxes has no cap.

### 4. Repairs and Maintenance ($2,000–$10,000 per year)

Every repair made to keep the property in rentable condition is immediately deductible. This includes:
- Plumbing and electrical repairs
- Appliance repairs
- HVAC servicing
- Painting and minor patch work

Keep meticulous records. The line between a "repair" (deductible immediately) and an "improvement" (must be capitalized) is one of the most contested areas in rental property tax law.

### 5. Cleaning and Turnover Costs ($3,000–$15,000 per year)

Professional cleaning between guest stays is a 100% deductible rental expense. This is one of the clearest deductions in the code—there is no ambiguity, no personal use allocation needed.

### 6. Platform Fees ($2,000–$8,000 per year)

Airbnb's service fees (typically 3%) and Vrbo's subscription or commission fees are fully deductible as advertising/marketing expenses.

### 7. Insurance ($1,500–$4,000 per year)

STR-specific insurance premiums are fully deductible. Standard homeowner's insurance premiums are partially deductible (the rental percentage).

### 8. Professional Services ($500–$3,000 per year)

CPA fees for your rental property tax return, attorney fees for lease review or dispute resolution, property management consulting fees—all deductible.

## Lodging Taxes: Your Other STR Tax Obligation

Beyond income taxes, most short-term rental hosts in the US are required to collect and remit lodging taxes. These go by many names:

- Transient Occupancy Tax (TOT)
- Hotel Tax
- Short-Term Rental Tax
- Lodging Excise Tax
- Tourist Development Tax (TDT)
- Transient Rental Tax

Whatever the local name, the structure is similar: a percentage of gross rental revenue (typically 5%–18%) that you collect from guests and remit to the local government.

### Does Airbnb Handle This?

Airbnb has agreements with hundreds of jurisdictions to collect and remit lodging taxes automatically. When guests book through Airbnb in these jurisdictions, the tax is collected from the guest and sent to the government without any action required from the host.

But there are two critical limitations:

**1. Airbnb's coverage is not universal.** Many jurisdictions—particularly smaller cities, counties, and states—do not have agreements with Airbnb. In these areas, hosts remain personally responsible.

**2. Direct bookings are always your responsibility.** If you book guests directly (through your own website, repeat guest relationships, or other channels), there is no platform to collect lodging tax on your behalf. You must collect it yourself and remit it.

### Registering for Lodging Tax

Even in jurisdictions where Airbnb collects and remits on your behalf, you may be required to register with the local tax authority and obtain a lodging tax account number. This registration is separate from your STR operating permit.

Failure to register when required is itself a violation, even if Airbnb is paying the tax correctly.

### Penalties for Non-Remittance

Local tax authorities take lodging tax non-remittance extremely seriously. Consequences include:

- **Back taxes** for all periods of non-compliance (often 3–5 years)
- **Interest** on unpaid amounts (8%–12% per year, compounding)
- **Penalties** of 25%–50% of unpaid taxes
- **Personal liability** for the host individually
- **In some jurisdictions, criminal prosecution** for willful non-remittance

I have personally worked with hosts who faced six-figure lodging tax liabilities after years of assuming Airbnb was handling everything. The lesson: never assume. Always verify.

## STR Tax Reporting Requirements

### Form 1099-K from Booking Platforms

As of 2026, any booking platform that pays you $600 or more per year must issue you a Form 1099-K. This form reports your gross payment receipts and is simultaneously filed with the IRS.

This means: **the IRS knows how much you received from Airbnb before you file your tax return.** If you fail to report that income, the IRS computer systems will flag the discrepancy automatically.

The matching program (where IRS matches 1099s to tax returns) is sophisticated and well-funded. Tax fraud involving underreported rental income is a priority enforcement area.

### What to Do With Your 1099-K

The 1099-K reports your **gross** receipts—the total amount guests paid, before Airbnb deducted its service fees. You'll notice this is higher than the amount actually deposited in your bank account.

Do not simply plug the 1099-K amount into your tax return as income. You must:
1. Report the gross 1099-K amount as rental income
2. Separately deduct Airbnb's service fees as a business expense

If you just report the net deposits, you'll underreport income even if the net amount is accurate. The IRS matching will flag this.

### Schedule E Filing

Most STR hosts report rental income and expenses on **Schedule E (Supplemental Income and Loss)**. Schedule E flows onto Form 1040.

Each rental property gets its own section on Schedule E. You'll report:
- Gross rents received
- Advertising expenses
- Auto and travel expenses
- Cleaning and maintenance
- Commissions (platform fees)
- Insurance
- Legal and professional fees
- Management fees
- Mortgage interest
- Taxes (property)
- Depreciation
- Other expenses

The net result is either a profit (added to taxable income) or a loss (may be deductible subject to passive activity rules).

## Passive Activity Loss Rules and the $25,000 Exception

For most STR hosts, rental income is "passive" income. Passive activity losses (when rental expenses exceed rental income) cannot normally be deducted against other income (like wages or business income).

However, there is an important exception: the **$25,000 Passive Activity Loss Allowance.**

If you actively participate in managing your rental and your Adjusted Gross Income (AGI) is:
- **Under $100,000:** You can deduct up to $25,000 in rental losses against other income
- **$100,000–$150,000:** The $25,000 allowance phases out proportionally
- **Over $150,000:** The allowance is completely phased out

"Active participation" for this exception is not a high bar—it means you make management decisions (setting rent, approving guests, authorizing maintenance), not that you're physically doing the cleaning.

---

## The Real Estate Professional Exception

If you (or your spouse) qualify as a "real estate professional" under IRS rules, the passive activity loss rules don't apply to your rental activities. You can deduct rental losses against any income, with no dollar limit.

To qualify, you must:
1. Spend more than 750 hours per year in real property trades or businesses in which you materially participate
2. Spend more than half of your working hours in real property activities

For a dedicated STR operator managing 5+ properties, this qualification is achievable. But the IRS scrutinizes real estate professional claims closely, and you need contemporaneous time logs to substantiate the hours.

## Short-Term Rental Tax Planning Strategies for 2026

Here are the strategies generating the most value for my clients right now:

**Strategy 1: Manage Personal Use to Achieve Rental Property Classification**
If you're close to the personal use threshold, reduce personal use days. Every day matters. Days spent doing maintenance or repairs don't count as personal use—a significant planning opportunity.

**Strategy 2: Cost Segregation for New Properties**
If you acquired a vacation rental property in 2025 or 2026, commission a cost segregation study immediately. The first-year bonus depreciation opportunity is significant and time-limited.

**Strategy 3: The Short-Term Rental Loophole**
For STRs with an average rental period of 7 days or fewer, the passive activity rules work differently. If you materially participate (500+ hours/year managing the property), the rental income may be classified as non-passive, allowing losses to offset other income without the $25,000 limitation or real estate professional requirement.

**Strategy 4: Maximize Deductible Startup Costs**
In your first year of operation, document all startup costs carefully. Up to $5,000 is immediately deductible; the rest is amortizable over 180 months.

**Strategy 5: Timing Income and Expenses**
Consider the tax year timing of bookings and expenses. A booking made in December for a January stay: when is the income recognized? Consult your CPA on revenue recognition methodology.

## FAQ: Short-Term Rental Tax

**Q: Is my Airbnb income considered self-employment income?**
A: Generally, no. Airbnb income reported on Schedule E is rental income, not self-employment income, and is not subject to self-employment tax. However, if you provide substantial services to guests (like a hotel would—daily maid service, concierge, meals), the activity may be reclassified as a Schedule C business, making it subject to SE tax.

**Q: Do I need an LLC for my short-term rental?**
A: An LLC provides liability protection but doesn't automatically change your tax treatment. A single-member LLC is a "disregarded entity" and is taxed the same as a sole proprietorship. Multi-member LLCs file partnership returns. The decision to use an LLC should be based on liability exposure and state-specific tax treatment, not federal income tax optimization.

**Q: What happens if I get audited?**
A: The most common issues in STR audits are: failure to properly allocate expenses between personal and rental use, misclassification of improvements as repairs, and unsubstantiated mileage and travel deductions. Meticulous record-keeping is your best audit defense. Keep all receipts, bank statements, booking records, and a contemporaneous mileage log.

**Q: Can my spouse help manage the property without affecting the passive/active classification?**
A: The IRS generally allows spouses to combine their hours for the real estate professional test, but only if both spouses materially participate. For the $25,000 exception, you need to demonstrate active participation (management decisions), not necessarily physical labor.
