# Holiday Rental Tax Deductions: Every Legitimate Deduction for 2026

In over 30 years of working with holiday rental owners, I have seen the same pattern repeatedly: hosts focus intensely on maximizing rental income but give almost no thought to minimizing rental taxes. The result is they pay far more tax than necessary.

The IRS tax code is not designed to punish rental property owners. In fact, it contains some of the most generous deductions available to any type of business or investment activity. The problem is that most hosts don't know what they're entitled to deduct—or they deduct things incorrectly and trigger audits.

This guide is exhaustive. By the time you finish reading it, you will know every legitimate deduction available to holiday rental owners and how to claim each one correctly.

---

## The Golden Rule of Holiday Rental Deductions

Before diving into the specifics, understand the governing principle: you can deduct **ordinary and necessary expenses** incurred in the operation of your rental business.

"Ordinary" means the expense is common and accepted in the rental industry. "Necessary" means it's helpful and appropriate for your rental activity—it doesn't need to be indispensable.

The key constraint is **personal vs. rental use allocation**. When an expense serves both your personal enjoyment of the property and the rental activity, you can only deduct the rental portion.

**The rental percentage formula:**

Rental days ÷ (Rental days + Personal use days) = Deductible rental percentage

*Example:* A beach house rented 150 days per year, personally used 30 days = 180 total days. Rental percentage = 150/180 = 83.3%. You can deduct 83.3% of shared expenses.

## Category 1: Direct Rental Expenses (100% Deductible)

These expenses occur solely because of the rental activity. They are 100% deductible regardless of the personal/rental use split.

### Cleaning and Housekeeping Fees

Every cleaning performed between guest stays is 100% deductible. This is one of the cleanest (no pun intended) deductions in the rental property space.

Keep records of:
- Professional cleaning service invoices
- Cleaning supplies purchased solely for the rental property
- Laundry service for guest linens and towels

If you clean the property yourself, you cannot pay yourself a wage and deduct it. But if you hire someone—including a family member—and pay them market rate, that cost is deductible (and the family member must report it as income).

### Booking Platform Fees

Airbnb typically charges hosts a 3% service fee on each booking. Vrbo charges either a commission (typically 5% + 3% booking fee) or an annual subscription.

All of these platform fees are 100% deductible as advertising expenses or commission expenses.

Don't miss this deduction. On $60,000 in gross revenue at a 3% fee, you're paying $1,800 to Airbnb—every dollar of which is deductible.

### Guest Supplies and Welcome Amenities

Anything you provide exclusively for guests—toiletries, welcome baskets, coffee pods, cooking supplies left for guests—is 100% deductible. Keep your Costco and Amazon receipts.

Hosts often miss this category entirely. Over a full year of hosting, these supplies can total $500–$3,000 in deductible expenses.

### Damage Repair and Replacement from Guest Use

When guests damage your property and the costs aren't covered by platform protection programs or insurance, those repair and replacement costs are 100% deductible as rental expenses.

Document everything: photos before and after each stay, repair invoices, replacement receipts.

### Credit Card Processing Fees

If you accept direct bookings and process credit cards, the processing fees (typically 2.5%–3.5% per transaction) are 100% deductible.

### Reservation Management Software

Software subscriptions used specifically for managing your rental (PMS platforms, dynamic pricing tools, channel managers) are 100% deductible.

This includes compliance management tools like [PermitPal](https://permitpalpro.com) that you use to track permit renewals, night caps, and tax obligations.

### Professional Photography

The cost of professional photography for your listing is 100% deductible as a marketing/advertising expense.

## Category 2: Proportional Expenses (Deductible at Rental Percentage)

These expenses serve both personal and rental purposes. You deduct only the rental percentage.

### Mortgage Interest

For a property with a mortgage, the interest portion of your payments is deductible at your rental percentage.

*Example:* Annual mortgage interest = $24,000. Rental percentage = 83.3%. Deductible mortgage interest = $19,992.

Note: The non-rental portion ($4,008 in this example) may be deductible as personal mortgage interest if the property is a qualified residence—subject to the standard consumer mortgage interest deduction rules and limits.

**UK Note:** Following the UK's removal of the FHL regime in April 2025, UK holiday let owners are now subject to the same mortgage interest restriction as standard residential landlords—relief is limited to a 20% basic rate tax credit, not a full deduction. This is a major change that significantly increases the tax burden on leveraged UK holiday rental owners.

### Property Taxes

The rental percentage of your annual property tax bill is deductible as a rental expense. The remaining personal percentage may be deductible as an itemized deduction on Schedule A (subject to the $10,000 SALT cap for US taxpayers).

*Why this matters:* The SALT cap limits your personal property tax deduction to $10,000 total. But the rental portion faces no such cap—it's deductible in full as a rental business expense.

### Insurance Premiums

Your homeowner's insurance premium (or dedicated STR insurance premium) is deductible at the rental percentage.

**Important:** You should have STR-specific insurance, not just homeowner's insurance. Standard homeowner's policies typically exclude commercial rental activity. The STR insurance premium is fully deductible.

### Utilities

Electricity, gas, water, sewer, trash collection, and internet/cable provided to guests are deductible at the rental percentage.

**Tip:** If you install a separate utility meter for the rental portion of a property (e.g., if you have a primary residence with a separate rental cottage), you can deduct 100% of the rental portion's utilities without allocation.

### General Maintenance and Repairs

Routine maintenance that keeps the property in good working order is deductible at the rental percentage:

- Annual HVAC servicing
- Pool maintenance
- Gutter cleaning
- Exterior painting (to maintain condition)
- Pest control
- Landscaping (routine maintenance, not improvements)

**Critical distinction:** Repairs (deductible at rental %) vs. Improvements (must be capitalized):
- **Repair:** Fixing a broken window = deductible
- **Improvement:** Replacing all windows with energy-efficient models = must capitalize and depreciate

This distinction is frequently contested in IRS audits. If in doubt, consult your CPA before classifying major expenditures.

## Category 3: Depreciation (The Most Powerful Deduction)

Depreciation is the most powerful tax deduction available to rental property owners—and the most underutilized.

### What Is Depreciation?

Depreciation allows you to deduct the cost of your rental property (excluding land) over its useful life. The IRS assigns residential rental property a 27.5-year depreciation period.

This is a **non-cash deduction**—you don't spend money each year, you simply take the deduction mathematically.

### How to Calculate Annual Depreciation

**Step 1:** Determine the cost basis of your property (purchase price + closing costs + improvements)
**Step 2:** Separate the value of the land (land is not depreciable)
**Step 3:** Apply the rental percentage (if property has mixed use)
**Step 4:** Divide by 27.5

*Example:*
- Purchase price: $450,000
- Land value (from tax assessment): $100,000
- Building basis: $350,000
- Rental percentage: 83.3%
- Depreciable basis: $291,550
- Annual depreciation: $291,550 ÷ 27.5 = **$10,602 per year**

That's over $10,000 in annual deductions that don't cost you a single dollar in cash.

### Cost Segregation: Supercharging Depreciation

A cost segregation study is an engineering analysis that identifies which components of your property qualify for accelerated depreciation—5-year property (appliances, carpeting, certain fixtures) or 15-year property (landscaping, parking lots, exterior improvements) rather than the standard 27.5-year building depreciation.

Under the current bonus depreciation rules:
- 5-year and 15-year property acquired in 2026 qualifies for 60% first-year bonus depreciation (phasing down from 100% in 2022)
- This allows you to accelerate substantial deductions into the first year of ownership

**Impact of a cost segregation study:**
For a $500,000 vacation rental property, a cost segregation study typically identifies $75,000–$150,000 in assets qualifying for accelerated depreciation. At 60% bonus depreciation, that's $45,000–$90,000 in additional first-year deductions.

At a 32% marginal tax rate, this translates to $14,400–$28,800 in tax savings in year one. The study costs $3,000–$8,000. The ROI is exceptional.

### Depreciation Recapture: The Trade-Off

When you sell a depreciated rental property, the IRS recaptures the depreciation you've taken at a 25% rate (not your regular income tax rate). This is called "depreciation recapture."

This doesn't make depreciation a bad deal—you're paying 25% recapture tax later on money you deducted at 32%+ earlier. The time value of money advantage is real.

But you should factor depreciation recapture into your disposition planning. 1031 exchanges are one strategy for deferring both capital gains and depreciation recapture indefinitely.

## Category 4: Travel and Vehicle Expenses

If you travel to your rental property for legitimate business purposes, those travel costs are deductible.

### Qualifying Travel Purposes

- Conducting repairs or overseeing contractors
- Meeting with property managers
- Inspecting the property after guest stays
- Attending required local inspections or permit meetings

Personal trips to your vacation home—even if you also check on the property—are not deductible.

### How to Deduct Travel Expenses

**By car:** Deduct either actual expenses (gas, oil, maintenance, insurance) at the business-use percentage, or use the IRS standard mileage rate (67 cents per mile in 2024). Maintain a contemporaneous mileage log with date, destination, business purpose, and miles.

**By air:** Airfare is deductible for qualifying business trips. If a trip combines business (inspection, repairs) with personal vacation, you must allocate.

**Meals:** 50% of meal expenses during business travel are deductible.

**Hotels:** Lodging during business travel is 100% deductible. You cannot stay at your own rental property and claim a deduction for it.

## Category 5: Professional Services

### CPA and Tax Preparation Fees

The portion of your CPA's fee attributable to your rental property (preparing Schedule E, cost segregation analysis, rental-related tax advice) is a fully deductible rental expense.

### Legal Fees

Attorney fees for:
- Reviewing or drafting rental agreements
- Handling guest disputes
- Permit application assistance
- Eviction proceedings (if applicable in your jurisdiction)
- Setting up a business entity for your rental

All of these are deductible rental business expenses.

### Property Management Company Fees

If you use a property management company, their management fee (typically 10%–20% of gross rental revenue) is a 100% deductible rental expense.

### Home Inspection and Safety Inspection Fees

Annual safety inspections required by your jurisdiction, or voluntary inspections for maintenance purposes, are deductible.

## Category 6: Local Taxes and Fees as Deductions

Here's one that many hosts miss: the lodging taxes and permit fees you pay are themselves deductible expenses.

### Short-Term Rental Permit Fees

Annual STR permit fees paid to your city or county are deductible rental business expenses.

### Business License Fees

If your jurisdiction requires a business license to operate your STR, those fees are deductible.

### TOT/Lodging Tax (if Self-Remitted)

If you collect and remit lodging taxes yourself (on direct bookings), the administrative cost of doing so is deductible. The tax itself is a flow-through that you collect from guests, but any fees associated with tax remittance software or filing are deductible.

## Category 7: Home Office Deduction (For Qualifying Hosts)

If you have a dedicated space in your primary home that you use exclusively and regularly for managing your rental business, you may be eligible to deduct home office expenses.

The home office deduction covers:
- A proportional share of home mortgage interest or rent
- A proportional share of utilities
- A proportional share of home insurance
- Direct office expenses (office supplies, printer, desk)

For this deduction to apply, the space must be used exclusively for business. A corner of the dining room table doesn't qualify. A dedicated room used only for managing your rental business does.

## Deductions Hosts Frequently Miss

After 30 years in this business, these are the most commonly overlooked deductions:

1. **Continuing education:** Courses, books, and subscriptions related to STR management are deductible
2. **STR industry association memberships:** Annual membership fees to VRMA, NALSA, or similar organizations
3. **Telephone and internet:** The business-use percentage of your phone and home internet (for managing reservations, responding to guests) is deductible
4. **Furniture replacement:** When furniture wears out from rental use, replacement costs are deductible (either immediately or through depreciation depending on the asset)
5. **Key lockboxes and smart locks:** Installation costs are deductible; the hardware itself is depreciable
6. **Guest welcome book production:** Design and printing costs for your guest guide are deductible advertising expenses

## Deductions to Avoid: The Red Flags

**Don't deduct personal expenses as rental expenses.** The IRS specifically targets mixed-use properties for audit because the personal/rental allocation is frequently manipulated. Keep a meticulous day-by-day calendar of personal use days vs. rental days. Claim only the legitimate rental percentage.

**Don't deduct improvements as repairs.** Adding a deck, installing solar panels, or remodeling a bathroom creates a capital asset that must be depreciated—not expensed immediately. Improperly expensing improvements is one of the most common audit triggers.

**Don't claim unreasonably high mileage.** Every business mile claimed must be logged contemporaneously. Reconstructed mileage logs created at tax time are easily identified and disallowed in audits.

**Don't take the home office deduction if you don't have a qualifying dedicated space.** The home office deduction is audited frequently. Only claim it if you have a genuine, exclusive business-use space.

## Organizing Your Deductions: A Practical System

The best tax planning is done throughout the year, not at tax time. Here's a system that works:

1. **Dedicated rental bank account and credit card:** All rental income deposits and rental expenses flow through these accounts. At year-end, generating your expense totals is trivial.

2. **Cloud-based receipt management:** Use an app like Expensify or simply photograph and tag every receipt on your phone. Store in a cloud folder organized by tax category.

3. **Day-by-day use calendar:** Maintain a calendar that clearly marks: guest stay days, personal use days, maintenance/repair days (not counted as personal use). This is your audit-proof documentation for the rental percentage calculation.

4. **Annual summary:** In December, generate a full-year summary of income by category and expenses by category. Share this with your CPA in January rather than searching for receipts in March.
