# Holiday Home Tax Rules: The Definitive 2026 Guide for UK, EU & Global Hosts

The tax landscape for holiday home owners has undergone seismic changes in the past few years—particularly in the UK and the European Union. Whether you own a cottage in the Cotswolds, a villa in Tuscany, or a beach house in Cornwall, the rules governing how that property is taxed have become simultaneously more complex and more aggressively enforced.

I've been advising holiday home owners since the early 1990s, when the UK Furnished Holiday Letting (FHL) rules first started gaining real traction. In three decades, I've seen more enforcement actions, more unexpected tax bills, and more hosts blindsided by rule changes than I can count. This guide exists so you don't become one of them.

---

## Understanding "Holiday Home" vs. "Rental Property": Why the Distinction Matters

Before diving into the specific rules, it's essential to understand that tax authorities in the UK, EU, and beyond treat a "holiday home" differently depending on how the property is actually used. The key variables are:

- **How many days per year is the property available to let?**
- **How many days per year is it actually let to paying guests?**
- **How many days per year do you personally use it?**

The answers to these three questions determine which tax regime applies—and the differences in tax treatment between regimes can be enormous. In the UK, for example, qualifying as a Furnished Holiday Let (FHL) versus a standard rental property can mean the difference between claiming capital allowances and being denied them entirely.

## UK Holiday Home Tax Rules: The Furnished Holiday Letting (FHL) Regime

### What Is a Furnished Holiday Let?

A Furnished Holiday Let (FHL) is a specific category of rental property under UK tax law. Properties that qualify as FHLs are treated more favorably than standard rental properties because HMRC views them as a genuine business activity rather than passive investment.

To qualify as a UK FHL, your property must meet all of the following conditions:

**Condition 1: Available to Let**
The property must be available for commercial letting to the public for at least **210 days** in the tax year (April 6 to April 5).

**Condition 2: Actually Let**
The property must actually be let commercially for at least **105 days** in the tax year.

**Condition 3: Pattern of Occupation**
The property must not be occupied by the same person for more than 31 consecutive days for more than 155 days in the year. This prevents hosts from circumventing the rules by letting to a single person for the whole year.

If your property meets all three conditions, it qualifies as a UK FHL and receives significant tax advantages. If it doesn't meet all three, it's treated as a standard rental property.

### UK FHL Tax Benefits: What You Actually Get

The benefits of FHL status in the UK are substantial:

**Capital Allowances**
Unlike standard rental properties, FHL owners can claim capital allowances on qualifying expenditure on furniture, furnishings, equipment, and fixtures. This is equivalent to depreciation in the US system and can generate significant tax deductions.

Standard rental property owners (non-FHL) can only claim a 10% wear and tear allowance and cannot claim capital allowances on fixtures and fittings the same way.

**Finance Cost Relief**
Prior to the April 2020 changes that restricted mortgage interest relief for residential landlords to a 20% basic rate tax credit, FHL properties were exempt from these restrictions. FHL owners could still deduct 100% of finance costs.

**IMPORTANT NOTE FOR 2024 AND BEYOND:** The UK government announced in the Spring 2024 Budget that the FHL regime would be **abolished from April 2025**. This is one of the most significant changes to UK holiday home taxation in decades.

### The Abolition of the UK FHL Regime: April 2025 Onwards

Starting April 6, 2025, the UK Furnished Holiday Letting regime has been abolished. Properties that previously qualified as FHLs are now treated as standard rental properties.

The key practical consequences:

1. **Loss of capital allowances**: New capital allowance claims are no longer available for holiday let properties (though existing capital allowances may continue to be claimed)
2. **Finance cost restriction**: Mortgage interest is now restricted to a 20% basic rate tax credit, the same as standard residential landlords
3. **Business Asset Disposal Relief**: FHL owners could previously claim Business Asset Disposal Relief (10% CGT rate) on disposal. This is now gone for most properties
4. **No longer eligible for EIS/SEIS investment schemes**: The enterprise investment scheme benefits tied to FHL status are lost

For many UK holiday home owners, this abolition significantly increases their tax burden. Working with a specialized UK property tax accountant to restructure your affairs is now more important than ever.

## Non-UK EU Holiday Home Tax Rules

### France: Location Meublée Touristique

In France, short-term furnished rentals (location meublée touristique) are subject to specific tax rules depending on your annual rental income:

**Micro-BIC Regime (Annual revenue below €77,700):**
- You benefit from a 50% flat-rate deduction (abattement) on your gross rental income
- Effective tax rate is applied to 50% of your revenue
- Simple reporting, no need to track individual expenses

**Réel Regime (Annual revenue above €77,700 or by choice):**
- Deduct actual expenses: maintenance, depreciation, management fees, insurance, mortgage interest
- More complex but potentially more tax-efficient for high-expense properties

France also requires mandatory registration with the local mairie (town hall) and in some cities (Paris, Lyon, Bordeaux, Nice), active permit systems with quotas.

### Spain: Tax on Non-Resident Holiday Home Income

If you own a holiday home in Spain but are not a Spanish tax resident, you face two potential Spanish tax obligations:

1. **Non-resident income tax (IRNR)** on actual rental income received
2. **Deemed income tax**: Even if the property sits empty for part of the year, Spain taxes you on "deemed" rental income for the unrented periods (1.1% or 2% of the property's cadastral value)

The tax rate for EU residents is 19%; for non-EU residents, it's 24%.

Spain has also introduced aggressive new controls on tourist apartment registration. Properties listed on Airbnb without a valid tourist license number risk being delisted, and property owners face fines up to €60,000.

### Portugal: The End of the NHR and the AIMI

Portugal's Non-Habitual Resident (NHR) tax regime—which offered generous flat-rate taxation for foreign income—was significantly changed starting January 2024. New NHR applicants face different rules, and many of the benefits previously enjoyed by holiday home owners and digital nomads have been curtailed.

Portugal also imposes an Additional Municipal Property Tax (AIMI) on properties with a tax value exceeding €600,000 (combined). Holiday homes are included in this assessment.

### Germany: Holiday Home (Ferienwohnung) Tax Rules

Germany taxes holiday rental income as "Einkünfte aus Vermietung und Verpachtung" (income from renting and leasing). Key rules:

- A property is treated as a genuine rental if it is let for at least 75 days per year and the annual rental income exceeds personal use income
- Below this threshold, it may be considered a "Liebhaberei" (hobby), and losses cannot be deducted
- Depreciation (Abschreibung) is available at 2% per year of building value over 50 years

Germany also requires registration with local authorities and compliance with state-level STR regulations, which vary significantly by Bundesland.

### EU Short-Term Rental Regulation (Regulation 2024/1866)

The EU Short-Term Rental Regulation that came into force in 2024 establishes a harmonized data-sharing framework across EU member states. Key provisions:

- **Registration**: Hosts must register their properties with national/local authorities
- **Unique Registration Number (URN)**: Must be displayed on all listings
- **Platform reporting**: Booking platforms must report host activity data to national authorities
- **Data sharing**: Member states share STR data to improve compliance monitoring

This regulation doesn't set tax rates, but it dramatically improves authorities' ability to identify non-compliant operators. The days of hosting in the EU without registering are effectively over.

## Deductible Expenses for Holiday Homes

Regardless of jurisdiction, the principle of deducting legitimate business expenses from rental income applies broadly. Here is a comprehensive list of typical holiday home deductible expenses:

### Property-Specific Expenses

**Maintenance and Repairs**
All maintenance and repairs necessary to keep the property in rentable condition are deductible. This includes:
- Plumbing and electrical repairs
- Painting and decorating (to maintain condition)
- Garden and pool maintenance
- Appliance repairs
- Pest control

Note: Improvements that enhance the property's value (e.g., building an extension, installing new kitchen) must be capitalized and depreciated over time, not deducted in full in the year of expenditure.

**Cleaning Costs**
Professional cleaning between guest stays is 100% deductible as a direct rental expense.

**Property Management Fees**
If you use a property management company or letting agent, their fees are fully deductible.

**Utilities During Rental Periods**
Electricity, gas, water, broadband, and waste disposal costs attributable to rental periods are deductible. For periods where the property is both personally used and rented, you prorate based on rental days vs. total days.

**Insurance**
Holiday home insurance premiums, including public liability coverage, are deductible.

**Mortgage Interest**
Subject to jurisdiction-specific rules (see UK section above for current restrictions), mortgage interest is typically deductible against rental income.

**Depreciation / Capital Allowances**
Furniture, appliances, fixtures, and the building itself (in many jurisdictions) can be depreciated over time, generating annual deductions without any cash outlay.

**Advertising and Platform Fees**
Airbnb service fees, Vrbo subscriptions, photography, website hosting, and other advertising costs are fully deductible.

**Accountancy and Legal Fees**
The cost of professional advice directly related to your holiday rental business is deductible. This includes tax accountant fees, legal fees for lease reviews, and compliance consultation fees.

**Travel Expenses**
If you travel to the property for genuine business purposes (to conduct repairs, meet with management companies, or inspect after guest stays), the travel costs are deductible. Keep detailed records of the purpose of each visit.

## Capital Gains Tax on Holiday Homes

When you eventually sell your holiday home, capital gains tax applies to the profit. Rates vary significantly by jurisdiction:

**UK Capital Gains Tax on Holiday Homes (Post-April 2025):**
- Residential property CGT rates: 18% (basic rate taxpayer) or 24% (higher/additional rate taxpayer)
- With the abolition of FHL status, Business Asset Disposal Relief (10% rate) is no longer available for most properties
- UK residents must report and pay CGT on residential property within 60 days of completion

**Spain:** CGT rates range from 19% to 28% depending on the gain. Non-residents face a 19% withholding at source.

**France:** Gains on the sale of a French holiday home are subject to IR (income tax) at 19% plus social charges at 17.2%, totalling 36.2%, with sliding scale rebates for properties held over 5 years (full exemption after 30 years).

**US:** Capital gains on vacation rental property are taxed at long-term capital gains rates (0%, 15%, or 20% depending on income). Depreciation recapture applies at 25%.

## Inheritance and Estate Planning for Holiday Homes

Holiday homes create specific complexity in inheritance and estate planning because they are often high-value assets held in jurisdictions with different inheritance tax rules than the owner's country of residence.

In the UK, holiday homes in England, Scotland, Wales, or Northern Ireland are subject to UK Inheritance Tax (40% above the nil-rate band of £325,000).

In France, the "assujettissement aux droits de succession" (liability to succession duties) applies to property located in France regardless of the owner's nationality or residence. French succession duties can be as high as 60% for unrelated beneficiaries.

Proper estate planning—including the potential use of Société Civile Immobilière (SCI) structures in France or similar arrangements in other jurisdictions—is essential for holiday home owners who want to pass their properties to the next generation efficiently.

## Practical Steps for Holiday Home Tax Compliance in 2026

Based on my experience advising hundreds of holiday home owners, here is what you should do right now:

**Step 1: Determine your tax residency and domicile**
Your tax obligations in your home country and the country where the property is located depend fundamentally on your residency and domicile status. Get professional advice on this foundation first.

**Step 2: Register with the appropriate authorities**
Both in your home country (to report worldwide income) and in the country where the property is located (for local tax registration and, increasingly, STR registration).

**Step 3: Set up a record-keeping system**
Track all income (by guest, by night, by platform) and all expenses (by category, with receipts). Allocate between personal and rental use. This is most efficiently done with property management software.

**Step 4: Understand your prorating methodology**
When a property is used for both personal and rental purposes, expenses must be allocated. The IRS (US) and HMRC (UK) use different allocation methodologies. Make sure you're using the right one for your jurisdiction.

## FAQ: Holiday Home Tax Rules

**Q: I only use my holiday home personally—do any tax rules apply?**
A: Even if you never rent your property, you may face deemed income taxation (Spain), municipal property taxes (applicable in most jurisdictions), and potentially capital gains tax on eventual sale. Ownership itself has tax implications.

**Q: Can I claim losses from my holiday home against other income?**
A: This depends on your jurisdiction and whether the property qualifies as a genuine business activity. In the UK post-FHL abolition, holiday home losses are generally ring-fenced against future holiday rental income. In France, losses from the micro-BIC regime cannot be carried forward. In the US, passive activity loss rules significantly restrict loss deductions for non-qualifying investors.

**Q: What records do I need to keep for a holiday home?**
A: Booking records (all guest stays with dates and amounts), all expense receipts and invoices, bank statements, records of personal use days, maintenance logs, and copies of all tax returns filed. Keep records for a minimum of 6 years in the UK, 6 years in the EU generally, and 7 years in the US.

**Q: Does the country where my holiday home is located tax the income even though I'm not a resident?**
A: Yes. Almost universally, income from property located in a country is taxed by that country regardless of the owner's residency. A double tax treaty between your home country and the property country may provide relief (typically through a credit mechanism), but both countries have the right to tax that income.
